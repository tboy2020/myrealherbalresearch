About this archive

One man's notebook, opened up.

This began in 2015 as a place to document findings on herbal plants and their usefulness. For ten years the research lived in scattered notes and replies to readers who wrote in with real questions. It is gathered here so that it can be searched, read and checked — nothing is sold, and nothing is charged for.

Plants are recorded the way they are actually spoken of at home: the Yoruba name first, the botanical name beside it, the part used, and the traditional use as it was received. Where published sources say something about a plant, they are quoted and named so you can weigh them yourself.

A word before you begin

Everything here is shared freely, as traditional knowledge — the way these plants have been used and handed down. It is offered to help, not as a prescription or a diagnosis, and it has not been reviewed by any medical body.

Please hold it alongside a doctor's care, not in place of it. For serious things — stroke, diabetes, high blood pressure, ulcer, kidney trouble or a lump — keep seeing your doctor, and let them know about any herb you are using, since herbs and medicines can affect one another. It is best not to stop a medicine you have been given in order to try something here.

And a gentle reminder: natural does not always mean safe. Take extra care during pregnancy or breastfeeding, and with children. If something is severe or getting worse, please don't wait — get seen.

The long study

Stone breaker: the plant I have followed the longest.

Of everything in this archive, this is the plant with the most written about it — at home, in Brazil and Peru, and in the research literature. Here is the record, with the sources named.

Eyin Olobe · Stone breaker

Phyllanthus niruri / Phyllanthus urinaria · also chanca piedra, quebra pedra

A small herb, not a tree, that grows uncultivated around doorways and compounds. It is known across Yorubaland, Brazil, Peru and the Caribbean under different names that all mean the same thing: the plant that shatters stone. It is prepared as a decoction from the whole plant — leaves, stem and root.

  1. A tea of the leaves is taken for kidney stones and for urinary infections generally; the plant and its close relatives are used the same way by rural communities across Brazil and Amazonia. Researchers have proposed that an antispasmodic action from compounds in the plant may account for a real effect on stones, and the same plants appear active against viral hepatitis.Sousa et al., 1991, pp. 377–378
  2. In tropical herbal practice it is used to clear obstruction through the body by helping the system pass mucus and stones, and to support the liver and gall bladder by encouraging the flow of bile. It is also taken as a diuretic, for urinary complaints, to help clear uric acid, and — by report — in hypertension, diabetes, bronchitis and fever.Raintree tropical-plant database
  3. The actions recorded for it include dispelling mucus, strengthening the liver and gall bladder, and dispersing calcification. In the energetic framing some practitioners use, it is described as clearing damp heat, moving liver energy and increasing bile, with testing points noted for the liver, gall bladder and kidney.Traditional-medicine compilation
  4. Listed as anodyne, aperitif, carminative, digestive, diuretic, laxative, stomachic, tonic and vermifuge, with reported use for colic, diabetes, dysentery, dyspepsia, fever, flu, jaundice, kidney complaints, malaria and tumours. In testing the plant has shown antihepatotoxic, antispasmodic, antiviral, bactericidal, diuretic, fever-reducing and blood-sugar-lowering activity.Duke & Vasquez, Amazonian Ethnobotanical Dictionary
  5. Widely known as a diuretic for urinary infections and fevers. In Brazil a tea of the whole plant is a home remedy for painful kidneys; in the Bahamas, where it is called hurricane weed, the bitter plant is boiled for poor appetite, constipation and fever.Field literature
  6. The plant drew wider medical interest after it was found to strongly suppress reproduction of the hepatitis B virus — a pathogen linked to liver cancer and carried by hundreds of millions of people. A search of world herbal literature for plants used against jaundice and liver disease, run at the Fox Chase Cancer Center in Philadelphia, brought Phyllanthus forward as one of the most promising to follow up.Fox Chase Cancer Center
  7. In Peru its common name, chanca piedra, means “shatter stone” — the very idea carried by the Yoruba name — and physicians there have prescribed the feathery-leaved herb for kidney and gall stones.Reported from Iquitos, Peru
  8. A German pharmacist who came to Iquitos to buy the dried plant kept records of more than a hundred people who used it for gall and kidney stones. By his account the stones cleared in about ninety-four percent of cases within a week or two, the main effect noted being occasional cramping as a stone passed, and he judged the result lasting. Records of this kind are collected personal accounts, not a controlled trial.Maxwell, Witch Doctor's Apprentice

Note kept with the file: the sources above are traditional and observational. They are recorded here because they are what the literature says, not as proof of a cure.
